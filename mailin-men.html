THE MAILIN MEN

Rob McGrath (15) · Sam Howard (21)
Sometimes Delivers

Formerly the Men of Malin, now rebranded with an i in the middle and a postal theme built entirely around the admission that they only occasionally deliver. Self-deprecation as team identity, which is a choice.

They lost Portugal by one point. Level with the Underdogs after Salgados, two behind after Silves, and they actually won the final day at Alto by a shot and still came second. That is the kind of defeat that sits with you for twelve months.

This is not a glamorous pairing and they know it. Where the Sultans will try to win it with fireworks, the Mailin Men intend to win it by never having a hole where they score less than two points. Over 54 holes, that is a genuinely viable route to a trophy — and it is exactly how they came within a single point last time.

The golfer

One of the most decorated players in the group and, on last year’s evidence, arguably the best. Rob was the only man to break 90 in all three rounds in Portugal, posted the low round of the week with an 83 at Silves that included the only sub-40 nine anyone managed, and contributed 107 best ball points — twelve clear of the next man.

The style is calculated rather than exciting: 5-wood off the tee, precise 7-iron, tidy chip, tap-in par. The trademark is a wrist-flick pitch from 30 yards that has rescued more holes than anyone cares to count. He does not bomb drives and he will not go for a par 5 in two. On a hard hole where he has a shot, he will happily treat it as a par 5, walk off with two points and let everyone else make the mistakes.

The card backs it up in the least glamorous way possible: 16 pars across 54 holes, nearly double anyone else in the field, against only three blow-ups.

STRENGTHS

  • Consistency of the highest order — the numbers are not close
  • Short game from 30 yards in, the strongest in the group
  • Course management, and the discipline to accept a shot rather than chase
  • Mentally tough, and demonstrably better under final-day pressure

WEAKNESSES

  • Lacks the raw distance of Sam, John or Brett at their best
  • Perfectionism — can overthink technical changes mid-round
  • Frustration creeps in when the scoring does not match the effort
  • Now the lowest handicap in the field, so the shots have gone

The man

This past year has produced a quieter version. Less drinking, less socialising, and a shift away from playing sport himself towards coaching, developing young footballers across Sheffield with real success. Whether that focus sharpens the golf or whether the group misses the old McGrath is one of the tour’s open questions.

“He’s gone Lesbian.”

— Former playing partner

Dragon Tour outlook

His handicap leaves little room for error, and he knows it. Cut to 15 and giving shots to everyone, it could add extra pressure. Nefyn is the round that should suit him best: exposed, awkward and full of holes where a boring four is worth more than an ambitious five.

The golfer

High risk, high reward, and a reputation that the numbers only half support. Sam opened Portugal with a 91 at Salgados and 38 best ball points — the single best round any player produced all week, and the reason the Mailin Men were level at the top after day one.

Then it unravelled: 98 at Silves, and 110 at Alto including a back nine of 57 that ended the title challenge. A 19-shot range, the widest of anyone in the field. Zero birdies across 54 holes but twelve pars, and seven triple-bogeys or worse. When it works it is the best golf in the group. When it does not, it is expensive.

The driver is the whole story. Long, towering, capable of turning a par 5 into a wedge — and equally capable of finding a car park. The mid-irons are the underrated part of his game, and the 5-iron in particular is a club he will pull in any situation and trust completely.

The rumour worth taking seriously

There is talk that Sam may go with the 5-iron off the tee rather than the driver on the tighter holes. On a card like Nefyn, where the outward half is exposed and The Point has boundaries on both the 11th and 12th, that would transform the Mailin Men. It converts their biggest liability into a consistent anchor and makes the never-below-two-points strategy genuinely achievable.

STRENGTHS

  • Enormous when the timing is right — the longest hitter on his day
  • Steady mid-iron play, especially the 5-iron
  • Aggressive, confident decision-making that occasionally wins a hole outright
  • A fast starter — the 38 at Salgados proves the ceiling is real

WEAKNESSES

  • Sprays the driver badly when chasing distance
  • Short game falls away sharply after a heavy night
  • Fades across a tournament — 38 to 31 to 20 in Portugal
  • Seven triple-bogeys or worse, the joint-worst in the field

The man

The joker and the resident vegetarian, a fact that continues to irritate John at every meal. Infectiously positive, thrives in the nightlife, and entirely capable of turning a quiet pint into a dawn taxi. The shadow puppet performances are Misters folklore and the details are best left where they are.

Like John, he has taken up padel over the past year, which raises a legitimate question nobody can answer yet: does a padel swing help a golf swing, or quietly ruin it?

“If golf was scored on entertainment value, I’d be scratch.”

— Sambo

Dragon Tour outlook

The single biggest variable in the tournament. If the 5-iron plan holds and he gives Rob a partner who never wastes a hole, the Mailin Men will be very hard to beat. If the driver comes out on The Point with the sea on both sides, it could get ugly quickly. Round three is his historical weak spot, and round three is Conwy.
