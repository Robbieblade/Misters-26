The holders

THE CHAMPIONDOGS

Steve Hampshire (24) · Phil Bennett (21)
Woof woof

The holders. The Underdogs won Portugal by a single point and rebranded on the back of it, to a name that is neither especially catchy nor especially original — a point the rest of the field has made repeatedly and will continue to make.

The consensus is that the win was a fluke. There is a case for it: they had one round, the second day at Silves, where everything clicked at once, and a 49-point better ball that was the biggest single round of the week. It gave them enough in the bank to survive a poor final day at Alto, where they scored 35 and were outplayed by both other teams. They won it on Saturday and hung on through Sunday.

There is also a case against that reading, and it is the one the Championdogs should be making loudly: they were the only team to post a score above 40 twice, they had two players contributing rather than one carrying, and 126 points is 126 points regardless of how it was assembled.

What is undeniable is that they arrive with nine fewer shots between them than they had when they won. Phil is down six and Steve down three. Defending a title with a reduced allowance is the hardest job on the tour.

The golfer

A left-hander with a slice that has become part of his identity. When it is under control it is close to a weapon: the ball starts down the middle, peels gently back and lands soft. When it is not, it can take him two fairways over and occasionally into a different postcode.

Portugal was his best week in years and the numbers say the win was earned rather than lucky. He was the second-highest points scorer in the entire field with 95, behind only Rob. His 37 at Silves was the round that won the tournament. And crucially he never dropped below 29 in a round — no player was more reliably in the twenties-plus every single day.

The gross scoring was ugly, at 308 with only eight pars, but Better Ball Stableford does not care about gross. He turned a modest scorecard into consistent points, which is precisely the skill the format rewards and precisely why the fluke argument is weaker than people think.

STRENGTHS

  • The most reliable points floor in the field last year — never below 29
  • Deft touch around the greens; short game is his own top-ranked strength
  • Resilient. One bad hole rarely becomes two
  • Steady under pressure, and unbothered by a slow start

WEAKNESSES

  • The slice, when overcooked, is genuinely destructive
  • Eight pars in 54 holes — the scoring is grind rather than flair
  • Sometimes too casual on the short putts
  • Three shots have gone from a handicap that was doing a lot of work

The man

Excellent company, a known BBQ master and the calmest head in the group. Has a habit of disappearing mid-evening and resurfacing later, contentedly, somewhere nobody expected. Brought a new mentality to golf last year and it visibly worked.

It has been a year of change off the course, and there is a fair question about which way that cuts. It could be exactly the fuel that keeps the Championdogs’ name intact, or it could be the distraction that stops him reaching last year’s heights. He has answered that kind of question the right way before — the 2025 trip was itself a comeback after walking away from the game entirely.

“Left side, right side — you’re never truly safe.”

— Fellow competitor

Dragon Tour outlook

Two of the three courses run along the coast, which raises a specific and entertaining question: is he brave enough to aim that banana out over the sea and trust it to come back? The Point at Nefyn will ask it directly. Off 24 he still gets a generous allowance, and his floor is the highest in the group.

The golfer

The pedigree is real and often forgotten. Lessons as a child, played right through his teens, and a swing that genuinely looks the part when it is working. The equipment is immaculate, the courses on his CV are among the best money can buy, and every short-game gadget ever manufactured is somewhere in the bag.

Portugal produced his single best moment: 38 points at Silves, the joint-highest individual round of the tournament, on the day that won the trophy. It also produced the other side — a 112 at Alto and nine triple-bogeys or worse across the week, the most of anyone. Zero birdies, ten pars, and a card that swings from brilliant to bewildering.

The limitation has never been the swing. It is the mentality, and everyone including Phil knows it. The four-putt is not a theoretical risk in his game, it is a documented event.

STRENGTHS

  • A genuinely good swing with real technical grounding behind it
  • Bunker play — smooth, confident and consistent
  • Strong driving when the rhythm is there; sets up scoring chances
  • Produced a 38-point round under pressure on the day it mattered most

WEAKNESSES

  • Nine triple-bogeys or worse in Portugal, the most in the field
  • Putting can go badly wrong, and has
  • Overthinks the short game mid-shot
  • Susceptible to psychological warfare, and the rest of the field knows it
  • Six shots gone — the biggest cut of anyone by a distance

The man

Stylish, generous, permanently good company, and capable of locating a hen party faster than a satnav. The luxury sports car of the group: high specification, not always road-ready.

He has moved into real estate this year and gone from financially comfortable to considerably more so. Which raises the only question that matters for a defending champion: can a man stay hungry when he is waking up on silk sheets?

“If money can buy it, he owns it.”

— Playing partner

Dragon Tour outlook

The most interesting handicap situation in the field. Six shots is an enormous cut, and the 38 at Silves is the round that justifies it — but he has to produce that level regularly now rather than once a trip. Off 21 he gets shots on the top three holes twice over, which is still a real allowance on cards as demanding as Nefyn and Conwy. If the head holds, he is a threat. If it does not, the Championdogs are defending with one man.
