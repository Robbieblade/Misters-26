How it was won

THE STORY OF THE TOUR

Friday — Porthmadog sets the tone

Two o'clock tee, four hours in the car behind them, and the Mailin Men opened with 44 points. The Sultans went with them at 42. The ChampionDogs, defending the title they won in Portugal, managed 35 and were already nine behind by the time they reached the bar.

Rob started with two blobs in the first five holes, then made a gross birdie at the 7th — a four at a par 5 for four points, the first of only four gross birdies anyone would make all week. Sam answered with a three at the 15th. That was the whole tournament in one round: the Mailin Men taking it in turns.

Saturday — Nefyn decides it

The Point did what an exposed clifftop does in a wind. The field averaged 24.4 points against 31.4 at Porthmadog. Five of the seven had their worst round of the tour. Twelve holes were picked up.

And the Mailin Men scored 44 again.

That is the tournament. Not the winning, but the not-losing — on the one day everybody else fell over, they posted exactly what they had managed on the easiest course of the three. The ChampionDogs went from 35 to 27. The Sultans from 42 to 36. The gap opened from nine to twenty-five and never closed.

“Never a hole below a two. Signed for, sealed and coming through.”

— The plan, as written before the tour

Sunday — too late, but glorious

Conwy produced the best golf of the week and it changed nothing. The ChampionDogs finally clicked and posted 44, matching the Mailin Men's best round of the tour. Steve Hampshire shot 93 for 39 points, the highest individual round anyone managed across three days.

They had needed that round on Saturday. Having it on Sunday, twenty-five points adrift, only made the arithmetic crueller: their ceiling was identical to the champions'. They simply did not visit it often enough.

The Mailin Men closed with 41, never headed at any point across 54 holes.

Champions · 131 points

THE MAILIN MEN

Rob McGrath · Sam Howard
Sometimes Delivers. This time, comprehensively.

They said before the tour they would win it by never having a hole where they scored less than two points. That is precisely what happened, and the statistic that proves it is this: across 54 holes the Mailin Men had zero holes where neither partner scored. Both other teams had four.

They were the outright best team on 16 of 54 holes, double the Sultans and nearly triple the ChampionDogs. Rounds of 44, 44 and 41 make them the only team not to post a round below 40 — their worst round was better than either rival's average.

Rob “The Bandit” McGrath — 103 points, 267 gross

The best player in this field for the second year running, and the margin is not close. His 103 points were eleven clear of the next man. Seventeen pars or better, three more than anyone else. Seven zero-point holes against a field average of eleven.

His Saturday at Nefyn was the round of the tournament. An 84 on the hardest day, in a wind, while the man playing alongside him shot 122. Ten consecutive holes from the 9th to the 18th without dropping below two points, worth 23 points on their own. Gross pars at the 14th and 15th — stroke index 3 and stroke index 1, back to back.

On that day he was the sole counting score on 14 of 18 holes. Sam had a difficult afternoon and it cost the team nothing, because Rob covered nearly the whole round on his own. If you want one round that explains why the Mailin Men won the Dragon Tour, it is that one.

Not without holes in the card. Five of his seven blobs came on holes rated stroke index 12 or easier, where he receives no shot. A full stroke over par on par 3s across the tour. And the Conwy back nine went 48 after an outward 38, the only time his round came apart.

Sam “Sambo” Howard — 88 points, 304 gross

Friday was the best round of his tour and among the best of anyone's: 92 gross for 36 points, including a gross birdie three at the 15th. He and Rob finished day one level with each other and clear of the field.

Then the back nines went. Sam scored 52 points on front nines across the tour and 36 on backs — a 16-point drop-off, the steepest of any player. At Nefyn he picked up four holes. At Conwy he came home having picked up the last three.

Twelve zero-point holes, the most in the field, of which eight were pick-ups. That is the one number to attack: a hole not finished scores nothing regardless of how well you are playing, and it cost roughly fifteen points across three days.

None of which cost the team, because of who he was standing next to. That is the whole argument for the partnership — a fast starter alongside a man who never has a bad day means the floor never drops.

Second · 118 points

THE CHAMPIONDOGS

Steve Hampshire · Phil Bennett
The holders, and the most frustrating story of the tour

They posted 44 at Conwy — a round that equalled the champions' best. Repeat it three times and they finish on 132 and win the Dragon Tour.

Instead they went 35, 27, 44. That is 26 points below their own ceiling, easily the largest shortfall of the three teams. The Mailin Men finished three points below theirs.

They did not lose this tournament to anybody else. They lost it on Saturday afternoon at Nefyn, where they scored 27, had eight holes between them with nothing on the card, and watched a nine-point deficit become twenty-five.

The mitigation, and it is a real one: Brett was drawn with them for that round and delivered his best work of the week. Without the wildcard their Saturday would have been worse still.

Steve “Teevo” Hampshire — 77 points, 324 gross

Thirteen points at Nefyn. Thirty-nine at Conwy. A 26-point swing between consecutive rounds, a wider range than any player has produced across two years of these tours.

The Nefyn round was a 122 with three holes picked up, no pars, and four points from the entire front nine. By some distance the worst round of the tournament.

What he did on Sunday should not be forgotten because of it. Ninety-three gross at Conwy — the longest and hardest card of the three — for 39 points and the best individual round of the Dragon Tour. Five pars. Three separate four-point holes at the 6th, 11th and 12th. And a gross two at the 6th, the lowest score recorded by anybody all week, on a hole where Rob picked up.

Second in the field on points across the final round, and last but one across the tour. Both are true and neither cancels the other.

Phil “Philly 5-Star” Bennett — 75 points, 311 gross

Gross rounds of 103, 105 and 103. A two-shot range across three completely different links courses is the tightest consistency anybody produced and a genuine achievement. Points of 25, 24 and 26 tell the same story.

The problem is where that consistency sits. He finished last on points and made only four pars or better in 54 holes.

Eleven zero-point holes from play, the most of anyone excluding pick-ups, five of them at Porthmadog on the opening day. He and Steve wrote off 23 holes between them; converting even half would have been worth more than the entire margin between second and third.

There is a good player in there and the card knows it. A man who can go round three links courses within two shots of himself has control. What he does not yet have is a hole where he takes the course on.

Third · 116 points

THE SULTANS OF SWING

John Sitch · Adam Fermie
Neither boom nor bust — a slow fade

Boom or bust was the billing, and what happened was neither. They opened with 42 at Porthmadog, two behind the leaders and very much in it, then slid to 36 and 35. No collapse, no fireworks.

They were the second-best team by outright holes won, taking 8 of 54 against the ChampionDogs' 6. On the raw better ball they finished ahead of the ChampionDogs; it was the Brettini modification at Nefyn that separated them at the death.

The 50-point round talked about before the tour never came close. Their best was 42 on day one, and the ham-and-egg they needed happened less often as the week went on.

John “The Bloodhound” Sitch — 92 points, 285 gross

Second in the field on points and second on gross. A quietly excellent tour that will get no attention because he played for the team that finished third.

He was the only player to complete all 54 holes without picking up once. In a week where twelve holes were abandoned across the group, and where several rounds fell apart because of it, John holed out everything.

His Porthmadog round was extraordinary in shape: 12 points on the front nine, 23 on the back. A 39 home after a 52 out. Across the tour he scored 40 points on front nines and 52 on backs, the strongest finisher in the field.

The gross birdie at Nefyn's 4th — a four at a par 5 on the hardest day of the week — was one of only four all tournament.

Adam “El Fermador” Fermie — 78 points, 319 gross

Thirty points at Porthmadog was a genuinely good day. He finished fifth on points, ahead of both ChampionDogs, which given how the week was framed beforehand is a result.

One par per round, though. Three pars in 54 holes is the fewest in the field and it caps what he can do — a card built entirely on bogeys and doubles scores steadily but never spikes. His best hole of the week was a gross five at Conwy's 5th, stroke index 1, which was a fine piece of golf.

The back nines were the issue: 43 points on fronts, 35 on backs, with five zero-point holes at Conwy alone, three of them picked up over the closing stretch.

The wildcard · 90 points

THE GREAT BRETTINI

Brett Miller
The format worked

The first outing for the wildcard format did what it was designed to do. Brett rode with a different team each day, his score never appeared on the live leaderboard, and each pairing worked out afterwards what he had been worth to them.

His 36 points at Porthmadog was the joint-best round of day one alongside Sam. He finished third in the field on total points, ahead of both ChampionDogs, on no tournament golf whatsoever.

Nefyn is where he earned the name. Six holes where his score beat both of theirs, on the afternoon the ChampionDogs posted 27 and were, without him, effectively finished. Exactly the scenario the wildcard was invented for.

The cruelty of Performance Related Pay is that his payout settles on the winning team's card, and with the Mailin Men at Conwy he was used on three holes of eighteen. His best work went to a team that finished second. He will make that argument. The rule does not care, which was the entire point of the rule.

WHAT TO LOOK FOR IN 27

1. Can anybody stop Rob McGrath?

Two tours, two Player of the Tournament awards, 103 points and 107 points, gross totals of 267 both times. Nobody has finished within eleven points of him in either year.

The gap is not luck and it is not the courses. It is 17 pars against a field average of nine, and seven blobs against a field average of eleven.

His two weaknesses are visible on the card: par 3s, where he was a full shot over par across the tour, and the easy holes where he gets no shot — five of his seven blobs came on stroke index 12 or higher. A rival who wants to close the gap should study those two columns.

2. Do the ChampionDogs turn Sunday into Friday?

The most interesting question of the next tour. They posted 44 at Conwy, matching the champions' best round of the week. Their ceiling is not the problem. Their floor is.

Twenty-seven at Nefyn cost them the tournament, and eight blank holes in one round is the number to attack. If Steve can put his Sunday round in on the Friday, or Phil can convert some of his eleven zero-point holes into ones and twos, they win. It is genuinely that close.

3. Where do the back nines go?

The clearest pattern in the data, and it splits the field neatly in two.

Sam is the outlier at both ends: the best front-nine scorer in the field and the worst on the back. Sixteen points is more than the winning margin. If he holds his front-nine form for eighteen holes the Mailin Men are unbeatable; if the fade continues, they are catchable.

John and Brett are the opposite problem — both gave away roughly a dozen points before the turn that they proved they were good enough to score.

THE ONE NUMBER EVERYONE SHOULD CARRY

Twelve holes were picked up across the tournament. Sam eight, Brett five, Adam four, Steve three. A hole you do not finish scores nothing, and those pick-ups were collectively worth more than the gap between first and third.

Nobody needs to get better at golf to fix that. They need to hole out.
